PhotoDrop Test report 15 Sep 2026 Guest uploads · moments · downloads · load · stability · security

PhotoDrop at 10,000 Photos

Guests can add their own photos and short clips to an event from its QR code, until a date the photographer picks, and delete only what they uploaded. The whole platform was then pushed hard: 10,000-photo uploads, 110,000-photo galleries, hundreds of people at once, and parts of the system deliberately crashed mid-upload. It holds up. A second round added zip downloads, guest blocking, location stripping, self-sizing workers and batched scan counts, each measured the same way. Testing turned up 22 problems that would have hurt at a real event; all are fixed.

Will it crash?
No

10,000 photos went up in one go with none lost or doubled, and the API never went above 139 MB of memory. It carried on through a killed worker, a killed API, a Redis restart and a frozen database.

1,000 photos at a go?
55 min on 4G

About 28 min on good 4G or venue Wi-Fi, 6 min on office fibre. The uploader's connection sets the pace — the server spent 0.07 CPU cores on it.

Downloading?
7 min for 500

Download all gives one zip of the newest 500 (1.9 GB): ready in 88 s, then 5 min to save at 50 Mbps. Or tick just the photos you want.

Secure?
64 / 64 checks

Guests can only delete their own uploads, and their photos lose location data on upload. Owners can block a guest. Forged, oversized, disguised and after-deadline uploads are refused.

What guests get

Real browser (Edge, phone-sized) against the running app

The photographer turns on Guest uploads and picks a closing date. Until then, anyone holding the event's QR code or link can add their own photos and short clips. Before they add anything, the page tells them what it's for and that everyone with the link or QR code can see it. The gallery gets four tabs: Photos (the photographer's), Guest photos, Videos (every clip, from anyone) and Yours. Guests never make an account. Their browser keeps a private upload key, and that key is the only thing that can delete their uploads, so nobody can delete anyone else's. The photographer can remove anything, and block a guest who shouldn't be uploading.

To download, tap Select and tick photos, or Download all for the tab's newest 500 — on Yours that means everything this browser uploaded. Either way it arrives as one zip.

Clips can be up to 2 minutes. Location and device data are removed from everything guests upload. The screens below were captured by an automated browser run: 13 of 13 browser steps passed.

Before the second round the same 1,000 photos processed at 1.75 a second. Now the worker also cuts each guest photo's metadata out and writes the clean 3.9 MB file back to storage, which nets 1.6 a second (this run had WORKER_NICE=10 set, with nothing else running). Moments now keep up. From upload to visible, a typical photo took 4 min and a typical clip 75 s (slow end 5 min and 2 min). The worker ran 4 photo jobs and 4 clip conversions at once, sized from this laptop's 8 threads.

Sharing one machine. Here the worker shares the laptop with the API, so doing more at once costs the gallery: a typical viewer's first page took 3.3 s, against 1.7 s when one clip converted at a time. Running the worker at a lower OS priority (WORKER_NICE=10) brought that down to 1.1 s and every venue upload went through, but it starved processing: the last photo appeared 12 min after uploads stopped instead of 4 min, and 3 clips timed out. So it's off by default. For a busy event, give the worker its own machine.

Every stored photo and clip was processed. The waits a phone saw are not the app's alone, though: the API averaged 0.2 CPU cores, while the program playing the 1,350 phones was itself frozen for up to 14.0 s at a time (slowest 1%: 1.9 s), and the 196 failed connections were those frozen phones timing out. Storage, the database, the worker, video conversion and the simulator all shared four cores, so this run shows that nothing breaks under a crowd, not how fast a crowd is served. That needs the simulated phones on separate machines, pointed at production-like servers.

Security

Found by testing, fixed

in this change
  • Critical
    Big uploads would have failed partway through

    Every upload link was issued at the start and expired after 15 minutes. 1,000 photos on 4G takes close to an hour, so everything after minute 15 would have been refused. Links are now issued a dozen at a time, seconds before use.

  • Critical
    The photographer's own upload stalled at about 300 photos

    Each file cost one API call and the site-wide limit was 300 calls per 15 minutes. Confirmations are now batched (1,000 photos ≈ 200 calls in total), and uploads have their own per-person limit.

  • Critical
    Guests on the same Wi-Fi would have locked each other out

    The gallery allowed 120 requests per 5 minutes per IP address, and most guests at a venue share one address. The first version of guest uploads also allowed only 30 new guests an hour per address. Limits are now sized for a venue: 150 guests behind one address uploaded with 0 refusals.

  • Critical
    Behind a proxy, every visitor would share one limit

    The server never read the real client address behind nginx or a load balancer, so one busy gallery could throttle the whole site. New TRUST_PROXY setting.

  • High
    The gallery crawled when a few hundred people opened it at once

    Every photo link was freshly signed per view (~1 ms of CPU each, 150 per page). Signed once per 10 minutes now: 10× the throughput and zero failures, and browsers can finally cache thumbnails.

  • High
    A database stall could hang requests without limit

    Queries had no time limit. During a 15-second freeze requests waited up to 16 s, and a connection that died silently would have waited forever. Queries now time out after 15 s and broken connections are replaced; after the freeze the next request succeeded almost immediately.

  • Critical
    The faster counters first deadlocked at a busy event

    Found by the crowd test in the second round. One counter update locked the photographer’s row before the event’s, the reverse of the others, so uploads confirming while the worker cleaned guest photos deadlocked: hundreds of uploads failed and some clips gave up. Every counter now locks in the same order. With 18 concurrent writers for 15 seconds, the first version hit 17 deadlocks and managed 114 writes; the fixed one hits 0 and 6,965.

  • High
    Removing location data first slowed processing to a crawl

    Found by the 1,000-photo test in the second round. The first version re-compressed every guest JPEG to drop its metadata, which cost seconds of CPU per 12 MP photo: processing ran at about a fifth of its usual speed, and every photo lost a little quality. The metadata is now cut out of the file without touching the picture (about 32 ms, pixels identical).

  • High
    Two files with the same name overwrote each other

    The uploader matched upload links to files by file name, so IMG_0001.jpg from two cameras became one photo. Links are now matched by position.

  • High
    A photographer could attach another event's files

    Confirming an upload didn't check that the file belonged to the event, or that it was in storage at all. Both are checked now, and the size storage actually recorded is what counts against the quota.

  • High
    Production start command pointed at a file the build never creates

    npm start ran dist/server.js; the build writes dist/src/server.js.

  • High
    Every visitor who wasn't signed in caused a server error

    Each page load asks "am I logged in?". With no session, the server crashed on the missing cookie instead of saying no, so every QR scan produced a 500 and an error in the logs. Found by the browser test; it now returns a plain "not signed in".

  • Medium
    Switching gallery tabs quickly could show the wrong tab's photos

    A slow response for the tab you just left could land after the one you switched to and replace it; the Moments tab would then show photos. Found by the browser test. Responses for a tab you've left are now ignored.

  • Medium
    Opening a very large gallery re-counted every photo each time

    The tab badges counted all 110,500 photos on every visit (81 ms). Big events now reuse their counts for 30 seconds: 11 ms per visit, and 45 → 94 requests a second when 400 people open it at once.

  • Medium
    A removed photo was subtracted from the event's count twice

    Removing a photo lowered the event's photo count, and the trash cleanup lowered it again when it deleted the file for good, so counts drifted low over time. Found while rewriting the counters for bulk loads; the migration recounts every event.

  • Medium
    A crash at the wrong moment left a progress bar stuck short of 100%

    If the API died between saving a group of files and updating the batch counter, the retry saw them as saved and never counted them (reproduced: 950 / 1,000). Both now commit together.

  • Medium
    A Redis hiccup could leave photos unprocessed forever

    A photo saved at the instant the queue was unreachable never got a job, and a retry saw it as already saved. The worker now re-queues anything stuck for 15 minutes; the API-crash test exercised exactly this.

  • Medium
    Retries were counted as failures

    A photo that failed once and then succeeded still added to the batch's failed count. Only the last attempt counts now.

  • Medium
    Deploying the worker dropped the photos it was working on

    It exited immediately on shutdown. It now finishes jobs in flight first.

  • Medium
    Scan counts counted every scroll and download

    Each gallery request wrote to the QR code's row, which also made every guest of an event queue on one database lock. Only opening the gallery counts as a scan now, and those counts are written in batches.

  • Medium
    Editing an event's date failed, and anyone signed in could read any upload's progress

    The edit passed field names straight through as database columns; the progress endpoint never checked ownership.

  • Low
    Smaller fixes

    Hidden photos were still downloadable by direct link. Gallery links were written to server logs. Broken JSON got a 500 instead of a 400. iPhone HEIC files were accepted but could never be processed (the picker now makes iPhones send JPEG).

Still open

decisions & next steps
  • Before launch
    Give the worker its own machine, with room to work

    One worker processes 1.6 photos a second on this laptop, so a 10,000-photo import takes 1.7 hours, and on a machine shared with the API a busy event slows the gallery (see A busy event). Run the worker separately, two or three of them for large events — each sizes itself to its machine. Memory is the limit that bites first: one 12 MP photo needs about 250 MB while it's resized and a zip adds its upload buffers, so a 1 GB server gets its processes killed mid-job; 2 GB is a working minimum and 4 GB is comfortable.

  • Know
    Long clips take longer to appear at a busy event

    Converting a 2-minute clip took 83 s at 1080p and 2 min at 4K on this laptop; with 4 converting at once it handled 1.3 1080p or 0.7 4K clips a minute. Forty guests each sending a 2-minute 1080p clip is about 31 min of conversion on one machine like this; a worker with more cores, or a second worker, shortens that. Generated test clips encode more easily than real footage.

  • Know
    Blocking is per phone, not per person

    Guests have no accounts, so a block applies to the upload key in that browser. Someone who clears their browser can upload again. For a determined abuser, switch guest uploads off for the event; if that becomes common, uploads could ask for a one-time code by SMS or email.

  • Next
    Treat uploaded videos as hostile input

    Guests control the bytes ffmpeg reads. Run the worker in its own container with least privilege, and keep ffmpeg updated.

  • Optional
    Backstop for old zips

    The worker deletes zips 12 hours after they’re ready. A bucket lifecycle rule expiring archives/ after a day would catch any the worker missed while it was down.

How this was measured

Everything ran on one Windows 11 laptop: Intel i5-8365U (4 cores, 8 threads), 16 GB RAM. Postgres 16, Redis 7 and MinIO (S3-compatible storage) ran in Docker Desktop; the API and worker ran as Node 22 processes; the simulated phones, browsers and photographers ran on the same machine. It used a separate, throwaway stack on its own ports. The dev database, the dev bucket and the real S3 bucket in .env were never touched.

  • Upload and download times on real connections are calculated, not measured here. On this laptop the "internet" was a local cable. Each estimate combines the measured photo or zip size (3.9 MB a photo), the measured server time per file and the connection speed.
  • Server figures are conservative. The database, storage, worker and up to 1,350 simulated devices all shared four cores. In production those are separate machines, and storage is S3, not a laptop.
  • Read the typical and slow-end (95th percentile) figures. About 1 request in 100 stalled for 2–4 s at every load level, on both database and storage paths but never at idle: Docker Desktop's disk and network layer on this machine.
  • Test files: generated 12 MP JPEGs sized like phone photos; 800×600 JPEGs for the 10,000-file runs; 12-second 1080p clips; real photographs for thumbnail sizes; photos and clips carrying GPS and device tags for the location checks.
  • Before-and-after figures in the second round compare the same scenario run on the code before and after the change, on this laptop.
  • Two things the tests got wrong along the way, both caught and fixed before these results: the crash test's recovery check once started too late to see the database come back, and the single-IP venue case was missing until it was added (it found the guest-key limit).
